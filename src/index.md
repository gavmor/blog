## 2026-11-04: That's Part of the Game

<!-- slug: 2026-11-04-thats-part-of-the-game -->

On a high-performing team, critique is necessary. But my team is wary of pedantry, and I understand why. We all hate red tape; we need a buffer so we don't end up hating each other.

What if we made critique into a sort of a game? We could try some shared rules that allow people to separate critique from personal criticism, and change them together if they don't help. The point is to make it safe to speak honestly about the work, not to add red tape.

Because critique is not natural. It's not a casual thing we're really prepared for. In life, we generally don't critique our friends. It's not something we're naturally inclined to do or even prepared for.

To be pedantic is to be annoyingly precise or prescriptive. Oh, you've got to cross your t's and dot your i's, walk at the intersection, go the speed limit, park within the white lines. Somebody who brings all this stuff up is engaging in pedantry. They're being pedantic.

And it is true that an overapplication of discipline is pedantry. But at the same time, if you just rely on casual relationships between communicative, even empathetic adults, you're going to struggle. I struggle with finding a sustainable venue for critique and feedback that's not taken as personal attacks and being purely judgmental.

And definitely, on this team, a bunch of times I have been talking about a component of the system or some piece of machinery and not known who made it or who worked on it or who touched it, and had somebody jump out defensively and say, “Oh, but Gavin, at the time we didn't know...” “I didn't mean to do that,” or “I wasn't thinking that it would do that.”

I can understand that reaction. When you pour hours into building a complex system, it can feel like an extension of you. If someone points out a problem, it's hard not to hear a judgment of your work. That's why we need the shared rules: not to dismiss that feeling, but to protect each other while we look at the machine.

Because this isn't about judging you. This is about the machine that we all as a team should be able to share opinions about, regardless of who made it.

That's why in a critique, you don't respond to a critique. Like in an art critique, when you have a group of artists and you go around the room, you put up your art, people do the critique, and you, the artist, do not respond.

That's part of the game.

Maybe we can try a lightweight version to save time arguing in circles: “We're going to treat this machine like a painting on a wall for exactly ten minutes. For the first five, the builder can't speak; after that, the builder can respond.”

And I should go first. I'll put my own recent architecture on the board and say, “Have at it. I'm taking notes, and I won't say a single word for five minutes.”
